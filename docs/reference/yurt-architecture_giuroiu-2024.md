# Yurt: Architecture, History, Sustainability, Materials and Typical Prices

*Published June 7, 2024*

Yurts are traditional, round, domed shelters originating in Central Asia and used by nomadic groups. Yurts have been used for over 3,000 years, evolving to meet the needs of nomadic lifestyles. They are still widely used in rural Central Asia and have gained popularity in Western countries for their eco-friendly and minimalist appeal. Architecturally, yurts are defined by their circular floor plan, collapsible lattice walls, conical rafter roof, insulating felt coverings, central crown skylight, and ornate wooden door. These features create a versatile, portable shelter well-suited to nomadic lifestyles and harsh environments. Yurts are characterized by their circular shape, lattice wall, insulated fabric cover, conical roof, portability, off-grid capability, affordability, and communal space. These elements make them practical for various uses, from temporary shelters to full-time residences. Living in a yurt presents unique challenges, such as temperature regulation, moisture management, securing utilities, pest control, and limited space. Key rooms in a yurt include a multipurpose main room, kitchen, bathroom, bedrooms, and a mudroom. Historically and culturally, yurts hold significant importance, symbolizing unity, balance, and a connection with nature. The most common architectural style of yurts remains the traditional Central Asian style. Modern yurts, however, have seen design innovations such as improved materials, enhanced insulation, advanced framing, off-grid technology integration, and smart interior layouts. Common materials for building yurts include wooden lattice walls, roof rafters, durable outer fabrics, and standard construction materials for additional features. Yurts are particularly suited to younger professionals, singles, couples, small families, and seniors seeking simplicity and connection to nature. The typical price of a yurt ranges from around $7,000-12,000 (€6,500-11,000, £5,600-9,600) for basic models. Buying a pre-made yurt is generally cheaper than constructing one due to economies of scale and streamlined processes. Given their expertise in eco-friendly and efficient design principles, a sustainable architect is best suited to design a yurt.

## What is yurt?

A yurt is a round, domed shelter used by nomadic groups in Central Asia. Yurts originated thousands of years ago on the steppes of Mongolia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, and other countries. Nomadic herders traditionally used them and remain widely used, especially in rural areas. The typical size of a yurt ranges from 13-20 feet (4-6 meters) in diameter. Some larger yurts can be up to 33 feet (10 meters) in diameter. The interior space is very versatile and can comfortably fit a family. Yurts are almost always found in rural or wilderness settings, as nomadic peoples traditionally used them. Many rural Central Asian families continue to live in yurts and use them as their main residence. They can also be found at some campgrounds and nature resorts. Most authentic yurts are still located across the pastoral rural areas of Mongolia, Kazakhstan, Kyrgyzstan, and other Central Asian countries. The structure consists of a circular wooden lattice wall covered in felt, with a conical roof frame that slopes upward to a compression ring. The roof rafters radiate from the central ring, which often has a skylight. Yurts are designed for quick assembly and disassembly, making them highly portable and well-suited for nomadic peoples following their herds over the steppes.

## What is the history of yurt?

The origins of the yurt can be traced back over 3,000 years to the nomadic peoples of Central Asia, particularly Mongolia. These early yurts consisted of a collapsible wooden circular lattice wall frame covered in thick layers of felt to insulate against the harsh winters. No single inventor or first builder can be credited with inventing the yurt. Yurts evolved organically over thousands of years among the nomadic peoples of Central Asia, especially Mongolia, to meet their needs for a portable yet sturdy shelter. Yurts were adapted for the nomadic lifestyle on the Central Asian steppes. They could be quickly dismantled and transported using horses and yaks to follow the seasonal movement of livestock herds. Inside, yurts had room to fit an entire family and their possessions comfortably. A central wood-burning stove provided heating and cooking facilities, while the roof had a built-in smoke hole. The design of the traditional Central Asian yurt has changed very little over the past three millennia. The components and construction methods modern Mongolian nomads use today are identical to how their ancestors built yurts centuries ago. In Mongolia, yurts are known as “gers” and remain the most common shelter, especially in rural areas where about 40% of Mongolians live in gers. The yurt’s first introduction to the Western world came through the writing of European explorers like Marco Polo, who traveled across Central Asia. The first modern yurt company in North America was founded in the 1970s, sparking a renaissance of interest in this ancient dwelling. Today, over 30 yurt manufacturers operate in North America as these circular shelters gain broader appeal.

## What architectural features define a yurt?

Several architectural features define a yurt, including a circular floor plan, collapsible lattice walls, conical rafter roof, layers of insulating felt coverings, central crown skylight, and ornate wooden door. Firstly, yurts are defined by their circular floor plan, typically ranging from 13-20 feet (4-6 meters) in diameter. The circular shape creates a spacious interior without using extensive materials. Secondly, yurts have a collapsible wooden lattice wall structure made of interlocking wooden strips. The lattice wall pieces, known as khana, quickly assemble into a curved frame. The bendable and modular khana allow the yurt to be rapidly dismantled for transport or seasonal migration. Thirdly, the roof frame comprises wooden rafters radiating upward from the top of the wall to a central wooden compression ring. This domed skylight ring is considered sacred in the traditional Mongolian gers. Fourthly, layers of thick insulating felt cover the entire exterior of the wooden frame. The felt is made of sheep’s wool but can also use yak or camel hair. The felt insulates against harsh winter winds and summer heat while allowing smoke from the interior fire to escape. Fifthly, an open hole at the roof’s peak corresponds with the sacred compression ring. This crown opening is a smoke hole from the central wood stove below, allowing ventilation. Finally, a wooden door frame reinforces the main entrance of the yurt, typically facing south or east. The door is often brightly painted or decorated with spiritual symbols. These features create a versatile, portable shelter adapted to nomadic peoples and harsh environments.

The conical roof: The conical roof is another key characteristic of yurts Yurts have a gently sloping conical roof shape that sheds rain, snow, and wind. Their small size also reduces energy costs, making them an affordable housing option for people on a budget. Community: Community is another key characteristic of yurts with a circular floor plan and communal heritage that make yurts ideal for bringing people together in shared social, spiritual, and learning spaces. Yurts can be used as classrooms, meditation centers, or community centers, providing a unique and inspiring space for people to come together and connect.

## What unique challenges come with living in a yurt?

There are several unique challenges of living in a Yurt. Firstly, temperature regulation can be challenging in a yurt. The thick felt or canvas walls provide good insulation, and yurts have a large surface area that loses heat. In summer, yurts can overheat quickly in hot climates. Strategic window placement for cross ventilation and shade is critical. Secondly, condensation and moisture need consideration in yurts. The impermeable covers trap moisture inside, which can lead to mildew or mold if ventilation is insufficient. A moisture barrier on walls, breathable fabric selections, and proper site drainage help mitigate this issue. However, cooking, breathing, and other humidity sources make some condensation inevitable. Thirdly, securing basics like electricity and plumbing takes forethought in yurts. Running power lines, installing solar panels, or using a generator provides electricity. Fourthly, pests pose unique problems in yurts. Careful sealing, frequent checks, and non-toxic extermination methods are necessary. Additionally, small round spaces provide no corners for spiders to build webs, causing some species to drop from the ceiling unexpectedly. Finally, limited space for possessions and activities can challenge some yurt residents. Regular purging of unused belongings is essential. Creative storage solutions like beds with drawers and overhead nets make the most of the available area. Alternative spaces like a camp kitchen, outdoor patio, or secondary structure supplement the modest yurt interior. The challenges of living in yurts relate to temperature, moisture, utilities, pests, and confined quarters.

## What historical and cultural significance does yurt have?

Yurts have a history and cultural significance spanning thousands of years. Originating in Central Asia, yurts were developed by nomadic peoples as portable dwellings that could be quickly assembled, disassembled, and transported across the vast steppes. Traditional yurts consisted of a collapsible wooden lattice frame covered in heavy felt insulation, animal hides, or canvas, with a domed roof structure held up by tension.

Yurts were intrinsic to the lifestyle of nomadic cultures across Central Asia and Siberia, used by groups including the Mongols, Turkic tribes, and Siberian clans. For instance, the yurt was central to Mongolian nomads, with some 60% of Mongolia’s population still residing in gers (Mongolian yurts) today. Culturally, the yurt holds deep spiritual meaning in many Central Asian traditions. The domed roof symbolizes the heavens, while the floor represents the earth. Yurts create a sacred space connecting these realms, embodying core values of harmony, balance, and reverence for nature. Today, yurts remain an economical, eco-friendly housing solution drawing on centuries of refined design. They continue traditional nomadic principles of portability and sustainability.

## What is the most common architectural style for yurt?

The most common architectural style for yurts is the traditional Central Asian style, originating from nomadic cultures in Mongolia, Kazakhstan, and other parts of Central Asia. The conventional yurt style features a circular floor plan with a domed roof held up by bent wooden lattice walls and rafters. The roof has a compression ring at the top where all the rafters join. A round or octagonal skylight sits at the top to let in light. The walls and roof are covered with thick felt or canvas layers for insulation and weatherproofing. A wood stove is typically located in the center for heating. The door is made of wood and is located across from the entrance. This simple yet ingenious architectural style allowed yurts to be easily dismantled, transported and rebuilt, which was essential for the nomadic peoples that developed them. Though modern yurts incorporate some contemporary materials and amenities, most retain this efficient round style that has served nomads well for thousands of years. Its continued popularity demonstrates the timeless beauty and utility of the traditional yurt’s architectural style.

## What design innovations are seen in yurt?

There are several key design innovations seen in yurts. Firstly, modern yurts utilize innovative materials like weatherproof architectural fabrics for covers instead of the traditional wool felt. These high-tech fabrics are lightweight, durable, fire-retardant, and available in creative colors and patterns. Secondly, recent yurt designs employ improved insulation techniques on floors, walls, and ceilings. Multi-layer rigid foam, reflective barriers, and synthetic fiber batting help regulate interior temperatures. Some models even allow conventional insulation to be pumped into cavity walls for maximum efficiency. These innovations keep yurts comfortable in extreme climates. Thirdly, advanced framing techniques create versatile, modular yurt components for simplified transport and assembly. This portability allows yurts to serve as temporary shelters or seasonal homes in multiple locations. Fourthly, off-grid technologies now integrate seamlessly into yurts. Composting toilets, solar panels, wind turbines, and rainwater harvesting systems supply essential utilities sustainably. Appliance and lighting options also emphasize efficiency. Finally, smart interior layouts make small yurts feel spacious and comfortable. Loft beds, convertible furniture, abundant storage, open floor plans, and large windows maximize usable space. Blending portability and sustainability, contemporary yurts provide appealing shelters aligned with eco-conscious values. Their creativity, efficiency, and versatility explain the growing popularity of yurts as full-time homes.

## What materials are mostly used to build a yurt?

The most common building materials for yurt are lattice walls, roof rafters, central dome openings, durable outer fabrics like canvas for covers, and standard construction materials for accessories like doors, windows, platforms, and utilities. Firstly, the lattice wall of a yurt is made from wooden poles, such as willow, poplar, birch, or bamboo. These flexible woods allow the lattice pieces to be bent into shape during construction. The lattice wall pieces interlock to form the circular structural frame of the yurt. Secondly, the roof rafters are usually constructed from wood, with larger dimensional lumber like 2x4s or 2x6s. The rafters radiate from the central wooden compression ring and rely on tension cables and the lattice walls for support. Thirdly, the central dome opening and compression ring at the top are key components of laminated solid wood. Modern yurts use skylights and steel cables for added durability. Fourthly, the outer covering of a yurt relies on heavy, durable fabrics. These covers must be waterproof, fire-retardant, UV-resistant, and able to handle snow loads. Insulation, like wool batting, is often placed between layers of fabric. Finally, yurts require doors, windows, and potentially a raised wooden platform or deck. These additional construction materials create a complete, livable structure. Modern conveniences like electrical wiring, plumbing, or heating systems can also be incorporated. These materials allow the ancient structural design of yurts to be adapted into modern off-grid shelters.

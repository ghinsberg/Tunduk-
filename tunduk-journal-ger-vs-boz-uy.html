Boz Üy and ger: the same silhouette, a different building.

From across a valley you cannot tell them apart. Step inside, and the Kyrgyz yurt and the Mongolian one are not the same thing at all.

Tündük18 October 20266 min read

Say the word "yurt" and most people picture one round, felt-covered tent. In truth there are several, and the two most famous are cousins, not twins. The Mongolians call theirs a ger. The Kyrgyz call theirs a Boz Üy, "grey house" in old Turkic, for the colour of the wool felt that wraps it. From a distance, on a green pasture under a big sky, you would struggle to tell one from the other. The difference is not on the outside. It is in how the roof is held up.

The difference is in the poles

Look up inside a Boz Üy and you see the whole answer. The roof poles, the uuk, are curved. They spring from the wooden crown at the very top, the tündük, and sweep down in a gentle bow to meet the lattice walls, the kerege. The curve does the work. The dome carries its own load out to the walls and into the ground, so it stands on nothing but itself. There is no post in the room. There is no centre at all, only one unbroken circle of floor.

A ger is built on a different idea. Its roof poles are straight, and because straight poles cannot carry a dome on their own, two wooden columns, the bagana, are planted in the middle of the floor to hold the central crown, the toono, up from below. It is a sound, beautiful solution, perfected over a very long time. But it puts two pillars in the heart of the room.

One is a dome that stands on itself. The other leans on two posts in the middle of the floor. That single choice shapes everything you feel inside.

Why the missing posts matter

For a family on the move the posts are no trouble. For a room someone is meant to stay in and remember, they change everything. A Boz Üy gives you the full circle: a clear floor the eye can cross in one sweep, and a dome overhead that becomes the whole drama of the space. Nothing stands in the way of a bed, a table, a view through the open door. Every square metre is usable, which is simply not true when two pillars occupy the middle.

That is the quiet reason the Boz Üy suits a hotel suite, a retreat room or a restaurant floor so well. The architecture itself is the luxury. You do not have to decorate around a problem, because there isn't one.

What the two share

None of this makes one tent better than the other. The ger is a masterpiece, exactly right for the life it was built for, and we admire it without reservation. Both are triumphs of nomadic engineering: a frame of hand-shaped wood, walls and roof of sheep-wool felt, joints bound in wet rawhide that shrinks tight and needs no nail. Both pack down onto a few animals and go up again in a morning. Both have been refined over more centuries than any factory has existed. They are not rivals. They are two answers, from two peoples, to the same question of how to live well and lightly on the open land.

A living craft, not a museum piece

The knowledge and skills of making Kyrgyz yurts were inscribed on the UNESCO Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage in 2014, and in 2025 the listing widened to take in the Karakalpak yurt as well. These are not labels on something finished. The craft is still practised by hand in villages like Kyzyl-Tuu, on the shore of Lake Issyk-Kul, where the people who hold that knowledge are the same people who build the Boz Üy we ship. When you choose the real thing, you are choosing them.
